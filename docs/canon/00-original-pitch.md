# Extra Toppings — original design pitch (canon)

> **Provenance:** the founding design document for this project, reproduced
> verbatim as initial canon. Where later decisions deviate from it, the
> deviation is recorded in [`docs/canon/README.md`](README.md) — this text
> itself is not edited.

---

The strongest version is not "Dope Wars, but the menu happens to be pizza."
The pizza shop needs to be the actual machinery of the criminal operation:
its cash flow, delivery routes, employees, storage, customer traffic and
expansion all create both opportunity and exposure.

**Working title:** Extra Toppings

**Pitch:** Build a neighborhood pizza empire. Use its delivery network to
control the city's underground market. Keep the food good, the books
believable and the rival shops afraid.

## The three feelings the game should preserve

- **Temptation:** the Dope Wars feeling that one more run could solve
  everything—or destroy you.
- **Ownership:** the Fast Food Tycoon feeling that this is your restaurant,
  your menu, your staff and eventually your empire.
- **Paranoia:** every new employee, vehicle and branch makes you richer but
  gives rivals and investigators another way into your operation.

## How the two games fit together

| Classic mechanic | Hybrid version |
| --- | --- |
| Prices vary by district | Contraband markets shift across neighborhoods each day |
| Limited carrying capacity | Pizza vehicles, drivers and storage are shared resources |
| Loan shark and deadline | You inherit a failing pizzeria with a dangerous debt |
| Random price events | Concerts, busts, shortages, festivals and rival actions move both markets |
| Restaurant branches | Each shop becomes a legitimate business and distribution hub |
| Recipes and demographics | Your restaurant concept determines customers, delivery volume and cover |
| Criminal syndicate | Certain employees become drivers, enforcers, burglars or informants |
| Sabotage and raids | Rival shops and warehouses are physical tactical locations |
| Dirty-money laundering | Each branch can only conceal income proportional to its believable sales |

## The core structure

I would make this a turn-based strategy layer with short real-time tactical
missions.

A continuous clock would weaken the deliberate, compulsive rhythm of Dope
Wars. Instead, time advances when you commit to deliveries, meetings, raids
or business decisions. The restaurant's performance is simulated in shifts.

### Morning: prepare

You receive:

- Current neighborhood prices and incomplete market rumors.
- News that may affect demand.
- Supplier offers.
- Staff problems.
- Intelligence about rivals.
- Legitimate ingredient and inventory needs.

You adjust the menu, assign employees, purchase supplies and decide where
the day's deliveries will go.

### Service: operate and distribute

The pizza shop begins taking orders. Your dispatch board mixes ordinary
orders with coded underground customers.

Every driver has limited:

- Vehicle capacity.
- Shift time.
- Nerve.
- Driving ability.
- Loyalty.
- Knowledge of the operation.

A route carrying only illicit orders is efficient but suspicious. A route
containing twenty pizzas and three concealed drops is safer—but those
additional stops may cause cold food, refunds and poor reviews.

Early in the game, you personally accompany one route. This preserves the
embodied Dope Wars experience: you have product in the car, prices may
change before you arrive and every traffic stop matters. As the business
grows, employees run most routes automatically while you personally handle
the day's most dangerous or valuable job.

### Night: settle accounts

After closing, you:

- Count clean and dirty money separately.
- Pay staff, suppliers and debt.
- Decide where inventory and cash will be stored.
- Launder a believable amount through the restaurant.
- Repair damage.
- Gather intelligence.
- Negotiate with rivals.
- Conduct or defend against raids.

Then the city advances one day: prices change, rivals act and investigators
strengthen or lose their case.

## The pizza business must be genuinely important

The restaurant cannot be a decorative "front" that players immediately
ignore. It needs to be a competent little tycoon game on its own.

Players choose recipes, prices, staffing, décor, hours and delivery
policies. But they should set policies rather than manually cook every
pizza. The game is about decisions, not repetitive kitchen chores.

Most importantly, different restaurant strategies create different criminal
advantages.

- A cheap late-night slice shop produces heavy delivery traffic and access
  to nightlife customers, but little clean profit.
- A family restaurant makes excellent clean money and builds political
  goodwill, but suspicious late-night traffic is conspicuous.
- An upscale gourmet shop attracts wealthy contacts and high-value orders,
  but employees and customers expect tighter standards.
- A catering-focused branch can move large shipments inside legitimate bulk
  orders, but contracts create paperwork and predictable routes.
- A college-area shop offers enormous volume and easy recruitment but
  creates chaos, theft and frequent police attention.

A player should never simply ask, "Which pizza makes the most money?" They
should ask, "What kind of operation am I building?"

## Shared resources create the best decisions

Almost every upgrade should benefit both sides while introducing a new
danger.

- A walk-in refrigerator stores more ingredients—and more illicit inventory
  to lose in a raid.
- A larger delivery fleet reaches more customers—but requires more drivers
  who could be arrested, recruited or turned.
- Extended hours increase pizza sales and provide nighttime cover—but
  attract neighborhood complaints and patrols.
- Security guards deter raids—but make a family pizzeria look strangely
  fortified.
- A warehouse enables bulk purchasing—but becomes a vulnerable stash
  location.
- A second branch increases laundering capacity and territory—but adds
  rent, payroll and another point of exposure.
- Better accounting raises legitimate efficiency—but a skilled accountant
  may notice what is happening.

This is the connective tissue that would make the hybrid feel like one
game.

## The market

Each district should have four important characteristics:

- Legitimate customer demographics.
- Underground demand.
- Enforcement pressure.
- Rival influence.

Contraband prices change through understandable events, not entirely opaque
randomness. The morning paper might report:

- A stadium concert increasing pizza traffic and nightlife demand.
- A port seizure creating a severe shortage.
- A university crackdown driving business into another district.
- A rival warehouse robbery flooding the market with stolen supply.
- A heat wave reducing restaurant traffic but increasing activity after
  dark.

Players initially know only the prices in their immediate neighborhood.
Drivers, customers and contacts provide rumors about other areas. Opening
branches improves the accuracy of local intelligence.

Selling too much of one product should depress its price. That prevents a
single obvious route from remaining optimal and lets the player
intentionally disrupt a rival's market.

## Clean money and dirty money

Laundering should not be a simple slider.

Each restaurant has a credible revenue range based on its actual sales.
Putting slightly more money through the books raises mild suspicion.
Claiming that a nearly empty shop sold $40,000 in pizza creates evidence.

Clean money is required for:

- Rent and payroll.
- Property and legitimate financing.
- Public restaurant improvements.
- Advertising.
- Lawyers, licenses and civic influence.

Dirty money pays for:

- Contraband.
- Off-book workers.
- Bribes.
- Illegal weapons and equipment.
- Certain supplier relationships.

Dirty cash also exists physically. If it is in the office safe, it can be
stolen. If it is deposited too aggressively, it can become evidence. The
player must constantly decide whether money is safer in the economy, the
restaurant or a stash.

## Employees should become characters

Keep the statistics compact:

- Food
- Driving
- Nerve
- Loyalty

Then add one or two personality traits: greedy, principled, reckless,
connected, observant, indebted or ambitious.

Employees begin with different levels of awareness. A good pizza driver
might be completely innocent. The player can eventually "read them in,"
gaining a useful courier but creating another person who knows the truth.

Poor treatment could cause an employee to:

- Quit during a critical shift.
- Steal from the restaurant.
- Accept a rival's offer.
- Blackmail the player.
- Cooperate with investigators.
- Attempt to take over part of the operation.

Good employees should become genuinely valuable. Losing the driver who
survived twenty dangerous routes ought to hurt far more than losing a
generic unit.

## Rivals are the heart of the game

Every major rival should also operate a pizza business and an underground
network. They need recognizable strategies and personalities.

One might run excellent restaurants but avoid violence. Another produces
terrible pizza and survives entirely through intimidation. A third controls
suppliers. Another specializes in political influence and informing on
competitors.

Competition escalates naturally:

1. Advertising and price competition.
2. Employee poaching and supplier interference.
3. Sabotage and planted contraband.
4. Extortion and territorial demands.
5. Warehouse robberies.
6. Armed attacks on restaurants.

Rivals should remember what the player has done. A business relationship
could become a truce, a supply partnership or a multiyear vendetta.

They also need readable intent. A raid should rarely appear from nowhere.
The player might notice unfamiliar cars, missing employees, a sudden
protection demand or increased surveillance. That gives meaningful
defensive choices:

- Move the stash.
- Hire guards.
- Close early.
- Pay tribute.
- Set an ambush.
- Strike first.
- Deliberately leave a decoy warehouse exposed.

## Raids

This is the Fast Food Tycoon mechanic worth modernizing.

Raids would be short, top-down, pause-and-plan tactical missions using the
actual restaurant or warehouse layouts. The floor plan the player designed
for kitchen efficiency becomes tactical terrain at night.

Objectives could include:

- Steal inventory or cash.
- Destroy ovens or delivery vehicles.
- Recover a debt.
- Photograph or steal a ledger.
- Plant incriminating material.
- Rescue a captured crew member.
- Intimidate the owner.
- Hold the building long enough to empty its warehouse.

Violence should be possible, but immediately escalatory. Guns increase
citywide enforcement, attract stronger enemies and make future raids
deadlier. Quiet burglaries, intimidation and sabotage should often be more
effective than killing everyone.

A raid must never become the universally best business strategy. It costs
personnel, creates evidence, risks retaliation and may damage the
neighborhood whose customers you need.

Being raided should cause serious but recoverable damage—not an arbitrary
campaign-ending wipe. The player might lose stock, equipment, information
or several days of operation. Named crew members can be injured, arrested
or permanently killed.

## Heat and investigations

I would use two related systems:

- **Heat:** immediate attention in a neighborhood. It rises quickly and
  decays when operations quiet down.
- **The Case:** persistent evidence tying the larger organization together.

A reckless delivery may create heat. A seized ledger, cooperating employee
or traceable financial discrepancy strengthens the case.

High heat means patrols, searches and difficult routes. A strong case
produces warrants, coordinated raids, frozen accounts and eventually
prosecution. This is much more interesting than a single wanted meter
because the player can be notorious without authorities necessarily being
able to prove anything—or quietly build an empire while unknowingly
allowing evidence to accumulate.

## Progression

### Act I: The hustle

One shop, one car and a debt due in thirty days. The player cooks up a
respectable menu while personally making dangerous runs.

### Act II: The operation

Drivers, specialized employees, storage improvements and the first
additional branch. Rivals begin treating the player as a territorial
threat.

### Act III: The syndicate

Warehouses, multiple districts, political influence, major suppliers and
organized raids. The player becomes increasingly removed from individual
sales but increasingly vulnerable to betrayal and prosecution.

## Endgames

Winning should mean securing an exit, not simply filling a progress bar.

- Build a profitable legitimate franchise and abandon the underground
  trade.
- Eliminate or absorb the other syndicates.
- Become the city's protected political power.
- Sell the entire operation and disappear.
- Betray the larger organization in exchange for immunity.
- Lose control of the restaurants but escape with enough cash to start
  elsewhere.

## A representative day

A concert is announced downtown. Pizza demand will be enormous after
10 p.m., and underground prices are already climbing.

You have capacity for twelve deliveries per hour. Eight are legitimate.
Adding four covert stops is relatively safe. Adding eight could produce a
fortune—but pizzas will arrive late, customers will complain and your
driver will remain exposed in rival territory longer.

Meanwhile, you learn that a rival warehouse will be lightly guarded during
the concert. Raiding it could provide cheap inventory and cause an even
greater market shortage. But using your best driver and two guards for the
raid leaves your own restaurant almost undefended.

The player is now weighing food quality, market speculation, staff
allocation, territorial politics, tactical risk and long-term investigation
exposure—all because of one evening's delivery schedule. That is the game.

## The first playable version

I would resist building the entire empire simulation immediately. A strong
vertical slice would contain:

- One customizable pizzeria.
- Four city districts.
- Four contraband categories.
- Two meaningful rivals.
- Eight named employees.
- One delivery vehicle.
- A thirty-day debt deadline.
- Dynamic prices and city events.
- Clean and dirty accounting.
- One warehouse.
- Three raid objectives using the same tactical layout system.

No open-world driving, multiplayer or manual pizza-cooking minigame. The
prototype needs to answer one question:

**Does running the pizzeria make the underground trading more
interesting—and does the underground operation make every restaurant
decision more dangerous?**

If that works, the game has an identity. Everything else can grow from it.
